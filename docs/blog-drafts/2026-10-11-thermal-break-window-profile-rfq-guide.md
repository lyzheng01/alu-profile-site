## A thermal-break label is not a complete specification

“Thermal-break aluminium window” describes a broad product category, not one interchangeable system. Two quotations can use the same label while differing in opening type, section geometry, insulating-bar design, glazing capacity, hardware, seals, drainage and test evidence. Those differences affect fit, fabrication, whole-window performance and cost.

The practical goal of an RFQ is therefore not to ask only for a profile price. It is to give suppliers enough information to identify the system being quoted, state what is included, flag unresolved interfaces and provide evidence relevant to the project. This guide is for importers, contractors, fabricators and design teams comparing window-profile options before a purchase order.

## 1. Define the window and the project before comparing profiles

Start with the finished assembly. Record whether the project needs fixed, casement, sliding, tilt-and-turn, folding or another opening type. State the approximate opening sizes, number of panels, frame depth constraints, installation condition and whether the profiles will be fabricated locally or supplied as assembled windows.

Then describe the environment and required outcome: climate, orientation, coastal exposure, wind and rain conditions, acoustic goals, operating frequency, security needs and applicable project or market requirements. Do not turn these notes into an assumed rating. They are the basis for asking the qualified system supplier or project engineer what tests, calculations or documentation are appropriate.

If the specification includes energy targets, distinguish frame performance from whole-window performance. The glass, spacer, gas fill, frame, sash, seals and dimensions all influence the result. For North American projects, NFRC publishes ratings for specific certified window, door and skylight products; its directory includes values such as U-factor, solar heat gain coefficient and visible transmittance. Ask for documentation that matches the proposed product configuration, rather than treating a profile-only value as the rating of the completed window. ([NFRC Certified Products Directory](https://nfrc.org/certified-product-directory/))

## 2. Send a section drawing that exposes the interfaces

A catalogue image rarely contains enough information to confirm compatibility. Attach a dimensioned cross-section and identify the frame, sash, mullion, glazing bead, thermal barrier, gasket grooves, drainage paths and hardware channels. Include units, revision, wall-thickness requirements and tolerances for dimensions that affect assembly or function.

Mark the interfaces that matter: glass thickness and bite, gasket position, corner cleat or crimp arrangement, sash-to-frame clearance, locking points, hinges or rollers, screen tracks, fastener access and connections to adjoining profiles. If multiple sections form one system, send the matching drawings together and identify which parts must mate.

Do not assume that two sections are compatible because their overall depths look similar. A small change in a groove, bead, screw channel or barrier seat can affect tooling, machining, assembly or the hardware choice. If a dimension is still flexible, mark it as a proposal for review instead of leaving it ambiguous.

## 3. Specify the thermal barrier without over-reading one number

Ask the supplier to identify the barrier material and product designation, its cross-section and nominal width, how it is mechanically retained, and which process controls or records are available for the proposed system. Request the drawing or data sheet that applies to the quoted profile. Where the project depends on thermal or structural performance, ask what test reports, calculations or system documentation support the claim and whether they cover the same profile, assembly and glazing configuration.

Barrier width alone does not establish whole-window performance. The surrounding aluminium geometry, cavities, frame dimensions, glass, spacers, seals and finished-window size also matter. A wider barrier may change the section and its interfaces; it is not automatically the right choice for every opening. Treat any performance figure as configuration-specific and confirm its units, test method, product dimensions and scope.

For projects where the barrier-to-aluminium connection is important, ask how the supplier verifies the mechanical joint and what acceptance records can be provided for production. Avoid accepting vague phrases such as “high insulation” or “strong thermal break” in place of a defined system, drawing and evidence package.

## 4. Make glazing, seals, hardware and drainage part of the same review

List the required glazing build-up: pane count, nominal thickness, laminated or tempered requirements, coating or tint, cavity and spacer, and any limits on total glazing thickness. If the glass specification is not final, give the acceptable range and ask the system supplier to confirm compatible beads, gaskets and clearances for the proposed options.

Identify the hardware by function and operating conditions: handle and lock arrangement, hinge or roller type, opening restrictors, load or cycle expectations, corrosion exposure and any security requirement. Ask which hardware family is assumed in the quotation and whether machining, installation and adjustment are included.

For water management, ask the supplier to show the intended drainage and pressure-equalisation route in the system details. Confirm drain-slot positions, end dams, corner conditions, weep covers and whether site installation can obstruct the path. The drawing should make it possible to follow water from the glazing rebate to the exterior without relying on a generic “waterproof” statement.

## 5. Define finish and appearance controls

Specify the finish type, colour reference, gloss or texture, visible faces, coating requirements and any project-specific appearance limits. For anodised finishes, state the colour and appearance criteria and ask how batch variation will be reviewed. For powder coating, identify the required coating specification or market requirement if one applies; do not use “standard coating” as the sole acceptance criterion.

Agree on sample approval before production. A useful approval package may include a finish sample, a representative extrusion length, a corner assembly or a fabricated mock-up, depending on what the buyer needs to evaluate. State which surfaces are cosmetic, how scratches or shade variation will be judged, and how approved samples will be referenced in the order documents.

## 6. Request evidence for the complete claim

A profile drawing, material certificate, finish record and finished-window test report answer different questions. List the evidence required for the project and connect each document to a part number, revision, test configuration and acceptance point. If a supplier cites a rating, ask for the report or directory entry and confirm that it covers the quoted window type and relevant configuration. NFRC’s directory is specifically intended to look up ratings for certified products; its published product records show that ratings are tied to product details, not merely to the material category. ([NFRC directory search](https://cpd.nfrc.org/search/search_label.aspx))

For air, water and structural performance, use the project’s specified test method and applicable code. Ask a qualified façade or fenestration professional to confirm which tests and ratings are required for the target market. Do not infer that a profile is compliant because another size or window configuration has passed a test.

## 7. Compare commercial scope on an equal basis

Along with the technical offer, ask each bidder to state what is included: profile supply only, cut-to-length service, machining, thermal-barrier assembly, fabrication, glazing, hardware, packaging or shipment. List quantities by section and finish, prototype and recurring volumes, target order schedule, destination, packaging constraints and requested delivery terms.

For custom sections, ask for separate tooling charges, ownership and storage terms, trial and sample stages, expected approval gates, and how design changes affect cost or schedule. If profiles are supplied as long lengths, state the maximum length that can be unloaded and stored at the destination. If they are fabricated into frames, add packing dimensions, protection of finished faces, labels and receiving inspection requirements.

Use a comparison table with columns for “specified,” “quoted,” “excluded,” “supplier clarification” and “evidence.” This exposes a low quote that omits hardware or testing just as clearly as a technical mismatch. Keep assumptions visible until both sides approve them in writing.

## Copy-ready thermal-break window profile RFQ checklist

1. Project location, building use, exposure and applicable project requirements.
2. Window type, opening method, overall dimensions and quantity by configuration.
3. Dimensioned section drawings with revision, units, alloy/temper requirements and critical tolerances.
4. Frame, sash, mullion, bead and adjoining-profile interfaces.
5. Thermal-barrier material/design and the evidence requested for the quoted system.
6. Glass build-up, gasket, hardware, drainage and corner-assembly requirements.
7. Finish, colour, appearance criteria and sample-approval method.
8. Required calculations, test reports, certificates and configuration they must cover.
9. Machining, fabrication, assembly, inspection and acceptance scope.
10. Tooling cost, ownership, sample stages and design-change process for custom sections.
11. Packaging, maximum bundle or frame size, destination, unloading limits and delivery terms.
12. List of open items, responsible party and approval deadline before production release.

## Example: turning a vague request into a reviewable one

Consider a hypothetical buyer who asks for “thermal-break casement profiles, dark grey, 1,000 sets.” That request leaves the supplier to guess the section, opening dimensions, glass build-up, hardware, finish criteria, test evidence, set contents and destination requirements.

A reviewable request attaches the frame and sash drawings, states the opening sizes and quantity by configuration, identifies the finish reference and cosmetic faces, lists the proposed glass and hardware or clearly marks them for supplier proposal, and names the required project evidence. It also asks for a scope table separating profiles, barrier assembly, machining, fabrication, hardware, samples, packaging and delivery. The buyer can then compare exceptions and resolve them before approving a sample or order.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a thermal-break profile enough to guarantee an energy-efficient window?

No. The completed window’s result depends on the selected frame and sash, barrier, glass, spacer, seals, dimensions and assembly. Request evidence for the actual window configuration and the relevant performance measure.

### Should I choose the widest thermal barrier available?

Not by default. Barrier design is one part of a system. Confirm how it fits the section, glazing, hardware and assembly process, then review performance evidence for the required configuration.

### Can one test report cover every window size and opening type?

Do not assume so. Ask which product family, size, opening type, glass and hardware the report covers. Have the project’s qualified professional confirm whether it is representative of the proposed design.

### What should I send if my drawings are not final?

Send the current revision and mark it “for quotation/design review.” Identify fixed requirements separately from open decisions, such as glass thickness or hardware. Ask the supplier to list assumptions and proposed changes so they can be approved before tooling or production.

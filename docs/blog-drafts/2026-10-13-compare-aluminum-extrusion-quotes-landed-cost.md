## A lower price per kilogram is not always a lower project cost

Two quotations can show the same alloy family and nearly identical outside dimensions yet differ materially in price. One may use a heavier section, include machining, or quote a more protective export pack. Another may exclude the die, inspection, inland delivery or destination charges. Comparing only the unit price hides those differences.

The reliable method is to compare the same approved product and the same commercial scope, then calculate the cost at the point where you need the goods. For an aluminum profile project, that means checking the drawing and wall thickness, the quoted weight basis, finish and fabrication, tooling and minimum quantities, packaging and freight, and finally the destination-country import treatment.

This guide gives importers, fabricators and project buyers a practical way to make that comparison without assuming that a thinner wall is always acceptable or that a tariff rate is universal.

## 1. Start with one controlled drawing and revision

Send every supplier the same dimensioned cross-section, revision and units. Identify the overall envelope, critical interfaces, cut length, required straightness or other functional tolerances, and any dimensions that may not change. Mark proposed values separately from approved values. If the design is still flexible, ask suppliers to identify alternatives as options, not silently substitute them into the base quotation.

This is especially important for wall thickness. A catalogue picture or a nominal profile name is not enough to establish the actual section. Two profiles can share the same outside height and width while differing in web and flange thickness, corner radii, cavities, ribs and local reinforcement. Those differences affect material use, stiffness, die design, extrusion flow, machining allowance and how the profile fits the assembly.

Do not ask a supplier simply to “make the wall thinner to reduce price.” First identify the functional surfaces and load cases, then have the responsible designer review any proposed change. The lightest section is not automatically the right section: deflection, local buckling, fastener pull-out, vibration, impact, corrosion allowance and assembly loads may matter. A supplier can explain manufacturability and quote an option, but structural adequacy needs application-specific engineering review.

## 2. Check wall thickness and weight per metre together

For a fixed alloy and section, the cross-sectional area is closely related to mass per unit length. Reducing metal area can reduce the theoretical material content per metre, but the relationship is not a simple percentage discount based on one wall dimension. The full section geometry changes the area, and production yield, tolerances, cut lengths, process route and commercial terms also affect the quoted price.

Ask each supplier to state the quoted basis clearly:

- Is the price per kilogram, per metre, per piece or per completed assembly?
- Is the weight theoretical from the drawing or based on an actual sample or production measurement?
- What unit weight is assumed, and does it include machining chips, protective film, packaging or accessories?
- Is the order quantity based on net finished weight, gross shipment weight or a minimum batch?
- Which drawing revision and wall-thickness values does that weight correspond to?

If one quote is per kilogram and another is per metre, convert them using the same agreed unit weight. If quotes are per piece, confirm the finished cut length and any added fabrication. Never use shipping gross weight to compare the material rate: it may include pallets, cartons and protective materials. For budgetary comparison, record both the profile-only basis and the shipment basis separately.

Where wall thickness is critical, ask for the drawing to identify the nominal wall and the applicable tolerance or inspection method. A single minimum value without a measurement location, datum or agreed inspection rule can create disputes. For hollow or asymmetric sections, define the relevant wall locations clearly. If a proposed thinner design is being considered, compare a marked-up drawing, recalculated unit weight, functional review and sample approval as one change package.

## 3. Normalize alloy, temper, finish and fabrication

The alloy and temper must match the engineering requirement, not just the cheapest line in a quote. Ask suppliers to identify the exact alloy and temper offered, applicable material standard or agreed documentation, and whether the quotation covers the same condition. If the grade remains open, request a technically justified alternative for engineer approval and keep it separate from the compliant base offer.

Surface finish can change both cost and appearance. For anodizing, powder coating, electrophoretic coating or wood-grain transfer, specify the finish route, color reference, gloss or texture where relevant, visible-face requirements, masking areas and sample-approval process. State how the finish is inspected and whether the quoted price includes test records. Do not compare “anodized” or “black powder coat” as if those labels alone define a complete finish specification.

List each value-added operation: cutting, drilling, milling, tapping, punching, deburring, assembly, protective film, labeling and kitting. For machining, identify datums, hole or thread requirements, critical dimensions, burr expectations, and whether dimensions apply before or after finishing. A quote that includes cut-to-length and deburring is not directly comparable with raw extrusions supplied in mill lengths.

## 4. Separate tooling, setup and quantity assumptions

For a custom section, ask whether the die or tooling cost is included, charged separately or amortized into the unit price. Record who owns the tooling after payment, where it is stored, what maintenance or refurbishment may cost, and whether transfer to another supplier is possible under the agreement. Confirm what happens if the drawing changes or the first trial requires adjustment. Do not assume a quoted die charge automatically gives unrestricted ownership or transfer rights.

Compare the same quantity breaks and minimum order assumptions. Unit price can vary with batch size because setup, die changes, finishing batches, inspection and packaging are spread across different quantities. Ask for the price at your actual release quantity and, if useful, at a larger planned annual volume. Keep sample charges, trial runs and recurring production prices as separate lines.

Also clarify yield, overrun or under-run allowances, accepted quantity, scrap responsibility and whether price is based on ordered length or accepted finished pieces. These commercial conditions can matter more than a small difference in the headline rate.

## 5. Compare packaging, freight and delivery terms

Profiles are long and vulnerable to rubbing, bending, edge damage and finish marks. Ask what separates pieces, how bundles are supported, whether finished faces are protected, how bundles are restrained, and whether the packaging is suitable for the actual transport mode and expected handling. Confirm bundle length and weight, pallet or crate dimensions, marking, moisture protection where relevant, and any destination wood-packaging requirements.

Request freight using the same origin, destination, shipment mode, cargo dimensions, gross weight and delivery term. Clarify whether the quote includes pickup, export clearance, main carriage, insurance, destination handling, customs brokerage, port charges, inland delivery and unloading. Under Incoterms, the named rule and named place matter; do not infer the full scope from “FOB,” “CIF” or “DDP” written without a precise place and version.

A compact comparison should show factory price and logistics separately. That lets you see whether one supplier is cheaper at origin but more expensive after packing, freight or local delivery.

## 6. Treat import duty as a destination-specific line, not a guess

Customs duty cannot be determined from “aluminum profile” alone. The relevant tariff classification and rate depend on the importing market and the product facts. The declared product form, composition, degree of fabrication, intended use where the tariff rules make it relevant, country of origin, customs value, preference eligibility and current trade measures may all need review. Classification can also trigger requirements beyond the base customs duty.

The six-digit Harmonized System is used internationally, but national tariff schedules add further digits and measures. In the EU, classification under the Combined Nomenclature and TARIC is used to identify applicable tariff and other measures; TARIC includes trade-defence measures such as anti-dumping and countervailing duties. In the United States, the importer should check the applicable HTS classification and whether the actual goods fall within the scope of any relevant trade-remedy order. A code supplied by an exporter is a starting point for review, not a guarantee that the importing authority will accept it.

Do not copy a tariff rate from another country, another aluminum product, an old shipment or a search snippet. Ask the importer or customs broker to confirm the current classification, origin treatment, customs-value basis, any additional duties, and the taxes or fees that apply to the specific shipment. Keep duties, import VAT or GST, brokerage and port charges as separate lines because they may be calculated differently and may have different recovery treatment for the buyer.

Useful official starting points include the [U.S. Department of Commerce guide to HS codes](https://www.trade.gov/harmonized-system-hs-codes), the European Commission’s [tariff classification guidance](https://taxation-customs.ec.europa.eu/customs/common-customs-tariff-cct/tariff-classification-goods_en) and [TARIC database information](https://taxation-customs.ec.europa.eu/online-services/online-services-and-databases-customs/eu-customs-tariff-taric_en). For U.S. anti-dumping or countervailing duties, CBP says importers need to review the scope of the relevant orders; see its [guidance on checking AD/CVD coverage](https://www.help.cbp.gov/s/article/Article-1081?language=en_US). These sources are starting points, not a substitute for shipment-specific customs advice.

The buyer’s accounting treatment and tax recovery rules may change which lines are recoverable, so do not treat every cash outlay as the same permanent cost.

## Copy-ready questions for suppliers

1. Please confirm the quotation uses drawing [number], revision [revision], and identify any deviations.
2. What wall thickness is quoted at each marked location, what tolerance or inspection method applies, and what unit weight does this produce?
3. Is the price per kilogram, metre, piece or finished assembly? Please state the conversion assumptions and what is excluded.
4. Which alloy, temper, finish, fabrication operations and inspection records are included?
5. Are die, setup, sample, minimum quantity, overrun or under-run charges separate? Who owns and stores the die?
6. What packaging, shipment dimensions and freight scope are included, and what costs remain at destination?
7. Which product data and origin documents can you provide to support the importer’s customs review? The importer or broker will confirm classification and applicable duties.

## The practical rule

Compare quotations only after you have aligned the drawing, wall thickness, unit-weight basis, finish, machining, quantity, packaging and delivery scope. Then add destination-specific import charges using a current customs review. If the supplier proposes a lighter section, compare it as a documented design option with a revised weight and engineering approval, not as an unexplained discount.

## A profile size is not a compatibility standard

Names such as 2020, 3030, 4040 and 4545 describe nominal outside dimensions in many product ranges. They do not, by themselves, tell you the slot opening, internal cavity, usable thread engagement, centre bore, corner geometry or the accessories that fit. Two profiles with the same outside dimensions can have different slot details. Conversely, profiles with different outside dimensions may share some accessories within a specific, documented system. Neither possibility should be assumed from the series label alone.

That distinction matters when buying a kit rather than a single extrusion. A frame is only useful when the profile, fastener, bracket, panel retainer, end connection and assembly method work together. A nut that will not enter the slot, a bolt that bottoms before clamping, or a bracket whose holes do not align can turn a low-priced bundle into rework.

The practical rule is simple: treat compatibility as a set of verified interfaces, not as a marketing name. Check the selected item drawing, supplier data and, when fit is important, a representative sample assembly before approving the order.

## 1. Identify the exact profile and its slot

Start with a part number or a dimensioned cross-section, not just a family name. Ask for the current drawing revision and confirm which exact profile is being quoted. A useful drawing identifies the overall section and the interface dimensions that affect assembly. Depending on the design, those may include the slot opening, slot depth, internal cavity, centre hole or bore, wall geometry, corner radii and the faces intended for connection.

Also record the material and finish if they affect the finished assembly. Anodizing, coating, or a protective film can change the usable clearance at a mating surface. If the profile is cut, machined or supplied as a finished frame component, identify those operations and the inspection basis separately from the raw extrusion dimensions.

If you only have a sample, take clear photos of the end section and the slot, include a scale for orientation, and ask the supplier to identify the closest drawing. Photos help explain the request; they do not replace a controlled drawing or establish a production dimension.

## 2. Check the fastener as a matched set

For a T-nut or other slot fastener, confirm the full interface rather than asking only whether it is “for 4040.” Check how the nut enters and rotates or slides into position, which slot geometry it is designed for, the thread specification, the mating bolt, and the required access for tightening. The supplier should identify the component drawing or part reference that applies to the selected profile.

Bolt length deserves its own check. A bolt can be too short to provide the intended engagement, or long enough to bottom out before the joint is clamped. Check the complete stack: bracket or plate thickness, washer if used, nut design and available thread depth. Do not invent a minimum engagement or torque value; use the component supplier’s written recommendation for the exact fastener and application.

For drop-in, roll-in, hammer or sliding nuts, confirm the intended installation sequence and whether the fastener can be added after the frame is partly assembled. A component may fit the slot yet be inaccessible in the planned build order. Include tool clearance and any access cover or panel in the review.

## 3. Verify brackets, connectors and panels separately

“Connector” can mean several different parts: an external angle bracket, an internal corner connector, an end fastener, a joining plate, a hinge, a foot or a panel-retaining component. Ask what joint each part is intended to make and how it is installed. A bracket’s hole spacing, thickness, orientation and screw access need to suit the selected frame layout; a connector hidden inside a profile may require a particular bore or access opening.

For panels, check the panel thickness range, retaining method, gasket or strip if applicable, and whether the panel can be installed or replaced after the frame is built. For end caps, feet and wheels, confirm the mounting face, insert or screw interface, clearance and load information supplied for that component. These are different checks; one successful corner connection does not prove every accessory in a kit is compatible.

When an assembly combines two profiles, verify both sides of the joint. “Fits profile A” is not enough if the other end of the connector mates with profile B, a plate, a panel or a machine surface.

## 4. Be cautious when mixing series or suppliers

Some manufacturers publish compatibility within a named product system. That is useful evidence for the products and combinations they specify; it is not a universal cross-brand rule. A supplier may document a compatible slot family, profile grid and connector range. Another supplier’s similarly named profile may use different interface details or tolerances.

If you intend to mix parts, ask the supplier to confirm the exact combination in writing and identify the supporting drawings or test-fit record. Provide both profile references and the connector or fastener part number. If only one side has been checked, mark the other as unresolved rather than treating the connection as approved.

For assemblies that carry equipment, guard machinery, support a work surface or otherwise have safety consequences, fit is not a structural approval. The design owner or a qualified engineer should assess loads, spans, joints, anchoring and applicable safety requirements using verified product data. Do not infer load capacity from the profile series, a photo or a hand-fit sample.

## 5. Run a representative sample-fit check

Before ordering a large batch, assemble the smallest sample that exercises the important interfaces. That may mean one profile, the intended nut and bolt, a bracket, the end connector and a panel section. For a frame with several joint types, include a sample of each critical connection rather than testing only one corner.

Check that the fastener can be installed in the intended sequence without damaging the slot or finish. Confirm that mating faces seat as intended, holes align, the bolt tightens without bottoming, and tools can reach the fastener. Try the actual panel, gasket or retainer where those parts affect the assembly. If the design will be adjusted or disassembled, test that operation too.

Record the profile drawing revision and the component references used for the sample. Photograph the assembly and note any clearance, access or fit questions. Do not force a part into a slot to make a sample appear compatible. If something does not fit, pause and ask the supplier to determine whether the issue is the selected component, the drawing, a tolerance, a finish build-up or a sample mismatch. Agree corrective action before production release.

## 6. Make the RFQ specific enough to quote

Send the supplier the information that identifies both the product and the assembly. Separate confirmed requirements from questions that still need a proposal. A concise RFQ can include:

1. Profile part number, series, drawing revision and dimensioned cross-section.
2. Slot and mating-interface dimensions shown on the controlled drawing.
3. Required alloy, temper, finish, colour reference and cut lengths, where applicable.
4. Fastener type, thread, length, quantity and the profile reference it must fit.
5. Bracket, connector, panel or retaining-part references and the joint each one makes.
6. Assembly sequence, tool access, panel installation and any disassembly needs.
7. Machining, cutting, deburring or finish-protection requirements for finished parts.
8. Sample-fit parts and the person responsible for approval before a production order.
9. Quantity by part, packing method, destination and requested delivery terms.
10. Open questions, assumptions, evidence requested and the date each item must be resolved.

Ask the supplier to list inclusions and exclusions: profiles only, or a kit with specified fasteners and connectors; cut-to-length or machining; samples; packaging; and delivery. A quote that says “accessories included” is not sufficiently defined unless it names the parts and quantities.

## Example: a 4040 frame request that still needs clarification

**Known:** The buyer wants a frame based on a nominal 4040 profile and needs two external corner brackets at each of four corners. The application, overall frame dimensions and destination are provided.

**Still to confirm:** The selected profile drawing and slot details; the exact bracket and fastener references; bolt length and thread; whether the brackets clear the intended panels; tool access; finish; sample-approval method; and whether the supplier is quoting a profile-only package or a complete kit.

This request is not ready for a compatibility promise just because “4040” appears in both product names. Ask the supplier to map each bracket and fastener to the chosen profile drawing, then test a representative corner before releasing the full quantity. If the supplier proposes an alternative, record it as a proposal and review the interface rather than silently substituting it.

## Common assumptions to remove from the purchase order

- “All 4040 accessories fit.” Replace this with named part references and confirmed mating drawings.
- “The slot nut is standard.” State the intended fastener and the profile interface it matches.
- “The bolt is included.” List thread, length and quantity, or identify the supplier’s exact kit.
- “The bracket fits both ends.” Confirm each side of the joint and the assembly orientation.
- “The sample went together, so the frame is approved.” Record what was tested; structural suitability needs its own engineering review.
- “Accessories included.” Name every included item and quantity in the quotation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do profiles with the same 2020 or 4040 label always use the same accessories?

No. A nominal outside size does not define every slot and connector interface. Compare the actual drawings and component references, and test the intended combination if fit is critical.

### Can I mix T-slot profiles and connectors from different suppliers?

Possibly, but do not assume it. Ask for confirmation of the exact combination with supporting interface data. A general statement about one supplier’s system does not establish cross-brand compatibility.

### What should I send if I do not have a CAD drawing?

Send the clearest available cross-section, photos of the profile end and slot, any part label, and the required assembly. Ask the supplier to identify a matching controlled drawing. Keep proposed dimensions separate from confirmed product data.

### Does a successful sample fit prove the frame can carry the planned load?

No. A fit check confirms only the tested assembly interfaces. Load capacity and safety require application-specific design review using verified profile, connector, span and anchoring data.
